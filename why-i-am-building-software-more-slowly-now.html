Why I'm building software more slowly now

By Denis Tarasenko Jun 4, 2026

What I notice more and more in coding, and in real life too, is that simpler is always the better choice. Always. It takes some time to see what simplicity actually looks like because first you have to slog through the mud of bad architecture, long terrible functions, and things breaking as you take even the tiniest steps. You think, “it’s already as simple as it gets”, suffer a bit more with ugly code and then there comes a point where you get a chance to finally start doing things properly. That saving chance usually shows up when it’s a brand new project and you’re not limited by existing patterns you’ve felt so attached to, even though they’d been slowly killing your joy without you fully noticing. But here’s the real risk: if you start rushing through again, you might end up in the same pit, this time even more reinforced and harder to escape later. So it’s really important to give yourself permission to slow down, so you could create a system you’d be proud of.

I became a dev before the AI age (which I think is a superpower nowadays), and once AI penetrated our relatively peaceful world, I, as most other devs, grew quite addicted to it in terms of building stuff faster, and over time I started anxiously noticing that my coding skills began degrading. Tiny things that took no effort and time for me to fix before, now forced me to lean on AI. The worst part was that when I went to Youtube to watch some devs talk about AI, it turned out to be quite a common trend nowadays. I was scared because being an engineer was a huge part of my identity and I didn’t want to lose it. Eventually, I accidentally overdosed myself with agents, which was my saving chance, and so I decided to get back on track of coding entirely on my own, using AI only as a learning tool, not a crutch to fix everything. And you know what? It felt just right. I finally realized what I’d been missing all this time. I started feeling accomplishment, and everything made sense once again. I also became a 10x better engineer over the following months because I learned new patterns and fixed the compounding architectural mistakes I’d been having for years.

These days when I open my past projects and go to some React components or functions or API endpoints, I immediately feel an inner itch because there are so much overengineering and unnecessary complexity in there. And it takes just a couple of minutes for me to cut the code in half, make it reusable, testable, and scalable. Simplicity has become my obsession. That’s what keeps me coming back to my IDE every day and enjoying every second I spend there.

P.S. just remove things, not add them, and it’ll get simpler
